Project Overview¶

The City of Chicago is one of the largest municipal employers in the US, with thousands of employees working across many departments. But how are these salaries distributed across departments? And does the size of a department determine how big its budget is? Is it possible to build a model to predict a department's total budget from different features?

In this tutorial, we answer those questions through the combination of two publicly available City of Chicago datasets:

  1. Current Employee Names, Salaries, and Position Titles: Individual-level salary data, Source: data.gov text

  2. 2026 Budget Ordinance -- Appropriations: Department-level budget allocations, Source: City of Chicago Data Portal

This reproducible notebook addresses data ingestion & cleaning, exploratory analysis, feature engineering and predictive modeling. To follow along, click the source links above, download the two datasets and upload them to your content folder!

Salary Dataset Description¶

This dataset comes from the City of Chicago Data Portal and contains information about current city employees. It lists their department, position title, salary type, annual salary, hourly rate, and typical hours worked.

Important variables include:

  • Department: the city department the employee works in
  • Job Titles: the employee's official position
  • Annual Salary: yearly pay for salaried employees
  • Hourly Rate: pay rate for hourly workers
  • Typical Hours: expected weekly work hours
  • Salary or Hourly: indicates whether the employee is salaried or hourly

It supports our research questions by allowing us to compare salary distributions, departmental pay differences, and workforce size across departments.

Convert Hourly Wages to Annual Salary¶

Some employees are paid hourly. In order to make them comparable across employees, we estimate the annual salary using the equation:

Annual salary = Hourly Rate x Typical Hours x 52 Weeks

Once this is done, we further clean the data by dropping the typical hours column, as it will no longer be used.

In [64]:
#Convery hourly to annual salary using above equation
current.loc[
    (current['Salary or Hourly'] == 'HOURLY') & (current['Annual Salary'].isna()),
    'Annual Salary'
] = current['Hourly Rate'] * current['Typical Hours'] * 52

#Drop Typical Hours column
current = current.drop(columns=['Typical Hours'] , errors='ignore')

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error, mean_absolute_error, r2_score

# Features and target
X = merged[['employee_count', 'avg_salary', 'median_salary', 'budget_per_employee']]

    X,
    y,

    random_state=42
)

model = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),

)

# Import libraries
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error, mean_absolute_error, r2_score

# Features and target
X = merged[['employee_count', 'avg_salary', 'median_salary', 'salary_std']]

    X,
    y,
    test_size=0.2,
    random_state=42
)

# Scale the data
scaler = StandardScaler()

X_train_scaled = scaler.fit_transform(X_train)
X_test_scaled = scaler.transform(X_test)

Departments with unusually large budgets or specialized funding structures likely introduce substantial variability that KNN cannot effectively model using only salary and employee count variables.

The results suggest that departmental budgets are influenced by many additional factors not included in this analysis, such as operational costs, infrastructure expenses, overtime, equipment needs, public safety funding, and administrative priorities. Overall, the KNN model demonstrates that salary-related variables alone are insufficient for accurately predicting budget allocation patterns across Chicago departments.

Model Comparsion¶

The multiple linear regression model assumes a linear relationship between the independent variables and the target variable. Linear regression is highly interpretable, allowing us to understand how each feature contributes to budget predictions. The model was able to capture some broad trends in departmental funding, but prediction accuracy remained limited, especially for departments with exceptionally large budgets or unusual operational structures. On the other hand, the KNN regression model does not assume a linear relationship between variables and can potentially capture more complex patterns. However, because the dataset contained relatively few departments and several extreme outliers, the KNN model performed poorly and struggled to generalize during cross-validation. The model was highly sensitive to the structure of the data and produced unstable predictions across folds.

Overall, the multiple linear regression model performed better in terms of interpretability and consistency, while the KNN model demonstrated the limitations of non-parametric methods on small datasets with high variability. Both models suggest that salary and workforce characteristics alone are insufficient for accurately predicting municipal budgets. Additional variables such as infrastructure costs, overtime, capital projects, public safety expenditures, and administrative priorities would likely improve predictive performance in future analyses.

Data Limitations¶

This analysis has several limitations. First, the salary dataset captures employee compensation but does not include all possible costs that affect departmental budgets, such as equipment, facilities, infrastructure, contracted services, benefits, or overtime categories. Second, after aggregation, the model operates at the department level, which creates a relatively small dataset. This limits machine learning performance and makes the models sensitive to outliers. Third, some departments may have unique responsibilities that make direct comparison difficult. For example, Aviation and Water Management likely require large infrastructure budgets that are not explained by salaries alone.

Final Conclusion¶

In conclusion, the analysis shows that salary patterns and employee counts provide some insight into Chicago departmental budgeting, but they do not fully explain budget allocation. EDA revealed meaningful differences in salary distributions across departments, especially through median salaries, percentiles, and outliers. The Fire and Police Departments showed relatively high salary levels, while departments such as Aviation and Water Management demonstrated that large budgets may be driven by infrastructure and operational costs rather than salary alone. The modeling results support this conclusion. The multiple linear regression and KNN models both struggled to make consistently accurate predictions, especially for departments with unusually large budgets. This suggests that department budgets are shaped by more than workforce size and employee compensation. Future analysis would be stronger if it included overtime, benefits, capital spending, equipment costs, and service-level demand.
